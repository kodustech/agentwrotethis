Faros's Q3 2026 AI Engineering Report measured what happens to review coverage as AI output rises, and one number in it decides how the rest of this problem should be framed. In the same twelve months of telemetry, the share of pull requests merged with no review at all rose from 31.3% to 76.3%. Review did not get slower in that period. The queue outgrew the people reading it, and what got dropped was the review itself.

That changes the question teams usually ask. Asking how to review more AI-generated code assumes reading speed is the lever and that every change can get read. At the volumes in the published data, the constraint is coverage: which changes a person actually reads, and what the merge gate accepts when nobody did.

The size and count data is the setup for that. LinearB's [2026 software engineering benchmarks](https://linearb.io/library/ai-in-software-development), drawn from more than 8.1 million pull requests, put agentic pull requests at a median of 89 lines and 293 lines at the 75th percentile, against 157 lines for unassisted work at the same percentile. The same set waits: 17.6 hours at the 75th percentile before review starts, against 3.4 hours for unassisted changes. Smaller and more numerous, and slower to get picked up.

## What the volume data shows

The [Faros report](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-speed-trap-takeaways) covers the most recent twelve months of telemetry from 22,000 developers across 4,000 teams with high AI adoption. Its method statement is on the report page, and it labels its findings on agentic review as correlational.

Some of what it reports is about how much work is being delegated: 79% of developers in the set use at least one AI tool weekly, 65% of AI-generated code is accepted, agentic review runs on 50 to 80% of pull requests, and agents open 13 to 14% of pull requests at the leading edge.

The downstream numbers are where review shows up. Average pull request size is up 71.8%. Time in QA is up 300.6%, which the report calls the largest deterioration among its flow metrics. Restarts, meaning work sent back after it is well along, are up 66.7%. Monthly incidents are up 125.4% while incidents per pull request are up 14.5%, so an individual change is not much more dangerous and the total operating load is.

Then coverage: 76.3% of pull requests merged without review, against 31.3% six months earlier in the same dataset. The report's explanation is the one a reviewer would guess. When queues are long and delivery pressure holds, review gets reduced or skipped to keep work moving, and doing that often enough makes it the default.

## Why the unreviewed share grows

Review capacity is close to fixed from week to week. Arrivals are not. Once the arrival rate passes what a team can read, the excess has to go somewhere, and the choices are to review fewer changes, review each one less deeply, add reviewers, or raise the bar for what counts as reviewed. The first two are what happens when the decision is left to the queue, because skipping a review leaves no waiting time and nothing that shows up in a review metric.

A ranker in your review queue does not change that. A ranker decides the order changes are read in, and [what a ranker can and cannot see](https://agentwrotethis.dev/blog/what-your-review-queues-ranker-can-and-cannot-see/) is a separate problem from how many changes arrive. Ordering does not reduce the count.

Coverage can be a rule instead of an accident. Decide which classes of change get a full human read, which get checked against the requirement they claim to satisfy, and which may merge on an agent's approval. [Routing review depth by change type](https://agentwrotethis.dev/blog/reduce-review-noise-which-layer-each-reviewer-turns-down/) is the mechanism, and writing the rule down gives you a record of which changes were skipped and why.

## Why a requirement check makes partial coverage defensible

When only part of the incoming work gets a human read, the rest needs something that is not a human opinion. The useful substitute is the requirement the change claims to satisfy.

A reviewer reading a diff answers whether the code looks right, which is answered from scratch on every change and costs more as the diff grows. A reviewer checking a change against its requirement answers whether the code does what it was asked to do, and that answer is a finite list of items marked delivered or not. It grows with the number of requirements, which the task already fixes, and not with the size of the diff. For a stream of small agent changes, that is the difference between reading each change on its own merits and closing out a checklist.

The requirement has to reach the reviewer for any of this to work. Every tool that supports it takes the requirement from one of a few places, and the choice decides how much of the mechanism functions.

The first is the pull request text. CodeRabbit's [linked issue validation](https://docs.coderabbit.ai/issues/pr-validation.md) looks for references in the description using platform syntax such as `fixes #123` or `closes #123` on GitHub, and full tracker URLs for Jira and Linear. On Azure DevOps it also reads work items attached through the platform's Development interface, using up to 200 valid linked work items and dropping deleted or permission-restricted ones without discarding the rest. The assessment is one of three states: addressed, not addressed, or unclear. Comments on the issue are not analyzed, so a decision made in a thread is invisible to the check.

The second is the tracker, read by id. Kodus's [Business Logic Validation](https://docs.kodus.io/en/how_to_use/code_review/business_logic_validation.md) finds the task in the pull request title, branch name, or description, in that order, and reads it from a connected tracker such as Linear, Jira, Notion, or Git Issues. A reference counts only once the tracker returns a task with that exact id, which stops a title containing `UTF-8` or `gpt-5` from being read as a ticket. It checks up to three tasks per pull request, follows a parent and its sub-tasks, and reports each requirement as MET, PARTIAL, MISSING, CHECK MANUALLY, or NOT IN TASK, citing the file and line each row is based on. Without a tracker, the task text can be pasted into the command.

The third is no requirement at all. GitHub Copilot code review checks the diff against the repository's custom instructions and a review effort level, and its [configuration documentation](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/copilot-on-github/set-up-copilot/configure-code-review) describes no input carrying what the change was asked to do. The [concept page](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agent-capabilities/agent-powered-workflows-and-operations/code-review) does not describe one either, so from what GitHub publishes, whether Copilot checks a change against its task is unknown. That is a statement about the documentation rather than about the product's internals.

## Who may close a gap, and what the gate accepts

A requirement check produces a list of gaps. What a gap costs is a policy decision, and it decides whether the check does any work.

CodeRabbit's linked-issue assessment reports a state and does not document a per-requirement closure, so a reviewer who disagrees has the comment thread and the label mechanism. Slop Detection does not block a merge: when a pull request is classified as slop, a warning goes into the walkthrough comment and, on some platforms, a label. Kodus implements closure explicitly. A gap can be marked ACCEPTED by someone other than the pull request's author, with the required permission depending on the platform, and the acceptance holds on later re-checks while the task text is unchanged. Edit the task and the requirement has to be accepted again.

The difference matters at volume. If the author can accept their own gap, the check is advisory and the list gets cleared the first time a change is close to shipping. Requiring a second person turns the same mechanism into a decision with a name attached, which is the same question that governs [whose approval satisfies a merge requirement](https://agentwrotethis.dev/blog/which-ai-reviewer-approvals-satisfy-your-merge-requirement/).

| Reviewer | How the requirement reaches review | States reported | Who may close a gap |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CodeRabbit | Linked issues in the PR description (`fixes #123`, `closes #123`, tracker URLs); native linked work items on Azure DevOps; up to 200 work items | Addressed, Not addressed, Unclear | No documented per-requirement closure; Slop Detection labels and warns without blocking |
| GitHub Copilot code review | No requirement input described in its docs; checks the diff against repository custom instructions and a review effort level | Unknown from its published documentation | Unknown from its published documentation |
| Kodus | Task found by id in PR title, branch, or description and read from a connected tracker; up to three tasks per PR; task text can be pasted directly | MET, PARTIAL, MISSING, CHECK MANUALLY, NOT IN TASK, each citing file and line | ACCEPTED by a reviewer other than the PR author (write access on GitHub, Developer or above on GitLab, org member on Bitbucket, Azure Repos, Forgejo); persists while the task text is unchanged |

Two properties decide how the check behaves in practice. CodeRabbit re-checks a labeled pull request on subsequent reviews, so the same finding can reappear and clears when a completed analysis finds nothing. Kodus re-checks on the first review and then only on a comment or a forced review by default, with an option to re-check on each push while something is missing, and each re-check is another validation. That cadence is the cost side of the mechanism, and it is worth setting deliberately.

## What to measure so coverage does not fall silently

The unreviewed merge share is measurable from git and platform data, which is why it appears in the Faros report at all. Count merges where the only approval was automated or where no review activity occurred, and report it next to your review time. A team that only tracks review duration will watch that number improve while more changes merge unread, because a review that never happened has no duration.

Alongside it, [what your merge gate sees when the AI review never ran](https://agentwrotethis.dev/blog/what-your-merge-gate-sees-when-the-ai-review-never-ran/) is the other half of the picture. A gate that passes on an agent's approval and a gate that passes on no approval at all look the same in a deployment log.

## What to do first

Start with the coverage rule, because it costs nothing and it addresses the larger number. Name which classes of change may merge on an automated check, and put a requirement check behind the ones that do, so the automated pass has something to check against. Then look at what the agents are pointed at, since low-priority backlog work inflates the queue with changes nobody was waiting for. Buying a faster reviewer comes last, because reading time is the smaller term in the published split between waiting and reading.

One caution on the numbers. The Faros and LinearB datasets are large, they disagree in places, and neither is a controlled experiment. Faros says its agentic-review findings are correlational, and LinearB describes its report as correlational too. Use them to pick what to measure on your own repositories, and treat the magnitudes as someone else's context.
